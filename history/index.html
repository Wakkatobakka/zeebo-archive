ZEEBO / PLATFORM HISTORY

Making games for Zeebo

The console, its development environment, and the games made for it.

A console with mobile roots

Zeebo Inc. was formed by Tectoy and Qualcomm. Its console used Qualcomm mobile technology and delivered games through a built-in 3G connection instead of discs. At GDC 2009, the company presented a system intended for households in developing markets.[1]

The intended audience

In May 2009, CEO John Rizzo discussed an initial Brazilian rollout, followed by Mexico. The executives described a mix of established games and new titles suited to local audiences. Qualcomm’s Mike Yuen explained their approach to finding that content:[1]

Why BREW was chosen

Reinaldo Normand told UOL in 2014 that the team had considered Symbian, Java and Linux before choosing BREW. He recalled Nokia’s commitment to N-Gage, concerns about Java piracy, and the cost of adapting Linux. BREW offered an existing shop and copy-protection system. This was a commercial and distribution decision as well as a hardware choice.[2]

What the production team encountered

In the same interviews, André Penha described an operating system still carrying telephone functions that the console did not need. Stefano Arnhold recalled that hardware limitations made adapting games much more expensive than expected. Looking back, Normand regretted positioning the console against the PlayStation 2 while offering less power and fewer games.[2]

A programmer’s account from 2008

Carol Gomes wrote about Zeebo before its retail launch. During her Tectoy internship, she and colleagues had added multiplayer to an unnamed football game and a racing game. She remembered having to work through difficult source code. Her post also argued that original games and a route for independent developers would be important to the console’s future.[3]

The development environment

The May 2009 Developer Guide describes Zeebo software version 1.0 as based on Qualcomm’s BREW 4.0.2 SDK, with OpenGL ES graphics and IHID controller extensions. Its specifications list VGA television output at 640 × 480 in a 4:3 aspect ratio.[4]

Tools and testing

The guide covers memory management, controller input, 3D graphics, audio, debugging and game submission. It includes instructions for BREW AppLoader and Logger, loading builds from an SD card, and tuning television output. Developers’ individual accounts describe how they used this environment for particular games.[4]

The shop closes

In May 2011, Zeebo announced the end of its Brazilian operations and Zeebo Interactive Studios. Its statement kept the online shop open until 30 September, with reduced game prices during the remaining months. Purchased content would remain usable on the console. After that deadline, a working machine’s installed library mattered in a way it had not when owners could still buy another download.[5]

An original console insert

This undated Portuguese insert welcomed new owners and advertised the games, Boomerang controls and Z-Credits. VG Visual Vault published scans of both sides in October 2019.[6]

The surviving collection

OpenZeebo’s compilation notes are dated 23 October 2011. They map numeric application IDs to game and system-app names. The archive retains those IDs, the original package variants and the measured file fingerprints, alongside the game histories.[7]

Documenting the people as well as the software

Former Tectoy Digital employee Bruno de Paula later began researching the company he had worked for, interviewing former colleagues and presenting its history. Reporting from the History of Games conference in 2024, John Szczepaniak described de Paula’s account of the studio’s split into Tectoy Studios and Zeebo Interactive Studios. He also reported their discussion of enthusiasts preserving the released games and the difficulties of installing dumps on original hardware.[8]
